# Tai Chi Sword Grip Guide

Give the direct answer first, then the one fix that matches the user's situation. Default position: the jian is held one-handed, snug against the guard, anchored by the ring and pinky fingers, steered by the index finger and thumb — firm enough to keep, soft enough to stay supple.

## The short answer

- **Hold it like a small bird: secure enough to keep, gentle enough not to harm.** On a 1–10 pressure scale, most of the form lives at 4–6. Only the moment of a thrust or power release firms toward 7 — then the hand softens again immediately.
- **The grip has a division of labor.** The ring and pinky fingers anchor the handle (they do the holding). The index finger and thumb form a soft ring near the guard and steer the blade's edge alignment (they do the aiming). The middle finger shares both jobs.
- **Hand position: tight against the guard.** The web between thumb and index finger (the "tiger's mouth") presses toward the guard, with the guard resting at the base of the index finger. Never grip mid-handle or near the pommel — every inch back makes the blade feel heavier and dulls point control.
- **Edge runs thumb-to-pinky.** With the blade vertical, the edge line points from your thumb side to your pinky side. You should always know which way the edge faces without looking.

## Triage: which situation is the user in?

| "My forearm burns / wrist aches after a few minutes" | Almost always a crush grip, not weak hands. See grip-pressure rules and fixes. |
| "The sword rotates or slips in my hand" | Grip is too loose or too far from the guard. Reset against the guard; anchor with ring + pinky. |
| "My movements look stiff / my teacher says my wrist is locked" | Loosen index finger and thumb first — they are usually the strangle point. Run the tap test. |

## The standard grip, step by step

1. **Place:** hold the sword in your dominant hand, blade vertical, edge running thumb-to-pinky. Slide the hand up until the guard rests against the base of the index finger and the tiger's mouth presses toward the guard.
2. **Anchor:** close the ring and pinky fingers around the handle — these two carry the sword's weight. The middle finger supports them.
3. **Steer:** lay the thumb and index finger into a soft ring near the guard. They do not squeeze; they sense and correct the edge direction.
4. **Hollow the palm:** keep a slight air gap in the center of the palm. A palm slapped flat against the handle transmits no feedback.
5. **Set the wrist:** wrist neutral and supple, blade edge aligned with the plane of the intended movement. No cocking up or down.

## Grip pressure rules

- **Default: 4–6 out of 10.** Enough that a partner could not pluck the sword from your hand; soft enough that your forearm muscles stay quiet.
- **Firm to ~7 only at the moment of effort** — the instant of a thrust (ci), a flick, or a power release — then release back to 4–6. The softening *between* movements is where skill lives.
- **Never 9–10 in practice.** A white-knuckle grip produces a stiff wrist, and a stiff wrist degrades every technique downstream.
- **Fatigue test:** with a proper-weight practice sword (roughly 400–600 g), forearm burn within the first ten minutes almost always means over-gripping, not under-training. Check pressure before blaming fitness. If pain persists after softening the grip, rest and consult your instructor or a healthcare professional.

## The crush grip — the mistake almost everyone makes

The most common error at every level is squeezing the handle like a hammer. The chain of damage: crushed handle → locked wrist → stiff forearm → broken flow. Fixes, in order:

1. **Soften the index finger and thumb first.** They are the usual strangle point; letting them go slack unlocks the wrist instantly.
2. **Tap test:** while holding the ready position, have a partner (or your free hand) tap the flat of the blade. The blade should give slightly and rebound — a rigid, welded-to-the-hand blade means too much tension.
3. **Freeze-frame wrist audits:** pause mid-form at random points and check — can you still wiggle the index finger? If not, you were crushing without noticing.
4. **Re-grip check:** the hand position should not slide or wander during the form. If you find yourself re-gripping, the anchor fingers are too loose while the steering fingers are too tight — rebalance, don't just squeeze harder.

## Drills that build a correct grip

- **Raise and lower (2 minutes):** raise the sword to shoulder height, lower it, and consciously soften the grip between efforts. This is the day-one drill and remains the reset drill for years.
- **Eyes-closed edge check:** point the sword at a door frame with eyes closed, then look — is the edge aligned with the movement line? This trains edge awareness through the steering fingers instead of the eyes.
- **Slow wrist circles and figure-eights:** keep the anchor fingers steady and let the wrist do the work. Any forearm tightness shows up immediately.

## FAQ

**How tight should I hold a tai chi sword?**
4–6 out of 10 for most of the form — firm enough to keep, soft enough to stay supple. Firm to about 7 only at the instant of a thrust or power release, then soften right back.

**Which fingers actually hold the sword?**
The ring and pinky fingers anchor the handle and carry the weight. The index finger and thumb form a soft ring by the guard and steer the edge. The middle finger assists both.

**Where should my hand sit on the handle?**
Tight against the guard, tiger's mouth pressed toward it. Gripping farther back makes the blade feel heavier and ruins point control.

**Is tai chi sword held one-handed or two-handed?**
One-handed, in the dominant hand. The free hand forms sword fingers and balances the movement — it never touches the handle.

**Why does my forearm get tired so fast?**
Crush grip. With a normal-weight practice sword (400–600 g), early forearm burn means you're holding at 9 when 5 would do. Soften the index finger and thumb first.

**Should my grip change during the form?**
Hand position stays fixed against the guard; pressure breathes — firmer only at moments of effort, soft again immediately after.

**What if the sword keeps rotating in my hand?**
Reset the hand against the guard and let the ring and pinky fingers actually close around the handle. Rotation means the anchor is too loose — usually because the thumb and index are doing all the squeezing.

## How to answer

- Lead with the one-line answer ("one hand, snug against the guard, ring and pinky hold it, index and thumb steer it, hold at 4–6 out of 10"), then the why, then the fix matching the user's situation.
- Give pressure as a number on the 1–10 scale — it is the fastest way to make "relaxed but firm" actionable.
- Ask at most one clarifying question — beginner learning the grip vs experienced practitioner fixing stiffness is the highest-value unknown. If unanswered, assume beginner and teach the standard grip.
- Reassure users with fatigue or pain: the problem is almost always technique (crush grip), not weakness, and it usually fixes within one or two sessions of deliberate softening. For persistent pain, recommend rest and consulting an instructor or healthcare professional.
- Keep the free hand (sword fingers) as a separate topic — mention it, don't teach it here.
