# Tai Chi Sword Techniques: How to Tell Them Apart

**Direct answer: tai chi sword is built on 13 core blade techniques (jianfa) — point (dian), thrust (ci), carry (dai), chop (pi), withdraw (chou), lift (ti), hit (ji), block (ge), clear off (xi), split upward (beng), stir (jiao), press (ya), and intercept (jie). To distinguish any of them, ask three questions: which part of the blade does the work (tip, edge, or flat), which direction the blade travels (in, down, up, across, or back), and what the wrist does (snaps, stays locked, or circles).**

Give that framework first, then the technique table — the framework is what lets the user identify techniques on their own.

## The three-question test

1. **Which part of the blade works?** Tip (point, thrust) — edge (chop, slice, sweep) — or the flat/body of the blade (hit, press, block). Most confusion disappears here.
2. **Which direction does it travel?** Straight in (thrust), downward (chop, point, press), upward (lift, split-up), horizontal (carry, stir), or backward toward the body (withdraw, clear).
3. **What does the wrist do?** A snapping wrist marks the point (dian); a firm, aligned wrist marks the thrust (ci); a circling wrist marks the stir (jiao).

## The 13 core techniques

| # | Technique (pinyin) | English | Blade part | Direction | One-line identifier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dian (点) | Point | Tip | Downward | Sharp peck with a wrist snap, like tapping a key |
| 2 | Ci (刺) | Thrust | Tip | Straight in | Linear stab, arm and blade in one line |
| 3 | Dai (带) | Carry | Edge | Horizontal, drawing back | Redirecting an incoming blade by carrying it aside |
| 4 | Pi (劈) | Chop | Edge | Downward vertical | Full-edge downward cut, like splitting wood |
| 5 | Chou (抽) | Withdraw | Edge | Backward out | Pulling the blade back out of contact |
| 6 | Ti (提) | Lift | Edge | Rising from below | Blade lifts upward, guard-high, defensive |
| 7 | Ji (击) | Hit | Flat or edge | Varies | Striking contact with the blade's body |
| 8 | Ge (格) | Block | Flat | Outward | Hard interception that stops an incoming line |
| 9 | Xi (洗) | Clear off | Edge | Sliding down/along | Wiping down the opponent's blade to clear it |
| 10 | Beng (崩) | Split upward | Edge | Upward flick | Explosive upward separation off contact |
| 11 | Jiao (搅) | Stir | Edge | Circles | Continuous circling that entangles the opposing blade |
| 12 | Ya (压) | Press | Flat | Downward | Sustained downward pressure on the opposing blade |
| 13 | Jie (截) | Intercept | Edge | Cutting across | Cutting across the attack's path before it lands |

Note on variants: lineages count the canon slightly differently — some lists include slice (liao) and sweep (sao) among the core techniques. The 13 above are the standard set as commonly taught with the 32-form tradition. The identification framework works for any variant.

## The easily confused pairs

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| Dian (point) vs Ci (thrust) | Point is a downward tip-peck driven by a wrist snap; thrust is a straight-line stab with a firm wrist and full arm extension |
| Pi (chop) vs Beng (split upward) | Same edge, opposite directions — chop travels down, split-upward flicks up off contact |
| Dai (carry) vs Chou (withdraw) | Carry redirects a blade that's still engaged; withdraw pulls your blade out of the engagement entirely |
| Ge (block) vs Jie (intercept) | Block stops force head-on; intercept cuts across the attack's line before it arrives |
| Ti (lift) vs Ya (press) | Lift rises from below to deflect; press bears down from above to control |
| Ji (hit) vs Dian (point) | Hit strikes with the blade's body or flat; point works only with the tip |

## How to practice distinguishing them

- **Call-and-response:** have a partner (or a video) call technique names while you perform only that technique slowly. Two minutes per session builds instant recognition.
- **Name-as-you-move:** run a section of the 32-form and silently name the technique inside each posture. Every movement in the form is one of these techniques in context.
- **Freeze-frame video check:** record one section, pause at random, and identify blade part, direction, wrist. If you can't name it, that's the technique to drill.
- **Learn them before the long form.** The 13 techniques are the alphabet; the forms are sentences. A week of technique drills saves months of unlearning sword-themed aerobics.

## FAQ

### What are the 13 tai chi sword techniques?
Point (dian), thrust (ci), carry (dai), chop (pi), withdraw (chou), lift (ti), hit (ji), block (ge), clear off (xi), split upward (beng), stir (jiao), press (ya), and intercept (jie). Lineage variants sometimes swap in slice (liao) and sweep (sao).

### How do I tell tai chi sword techniques apart?
Ask three questions: which part of the blade works (tip, edge, or flat), which direction it travels (in, down, up, across, back), and what the wrist does (snap, locked, or circle). Those three answers uniquely identify any technique.

### What is the difference between dian and ci in tai chi sword?
Dian (point) is a sharp downward peck with the tip driven by a wrist snap. Ci (thrust) is a straight-line stab with the tip, arm and blade aligned, wrist firm. Same blade part, different direction and wrist action.

### What does jianfa mean?
Jianfa (剑法) means "sword techniques" — the vocabulary of blade methods (thrust, chop, point, and so on) that every tai chi sword form is composed of.

### Which sword techniques does the 32-form teach?
Seven of the thirteen: point (dian), thrust (ci), carry (dai), chop (pi), withdraw (chou), lift (ti), and hit (ji). The remaining six appear as you advance into longer and competition forms.

### Should I learn the techniques before the form?
Yes — even one week of technique-only drills first pays off. The techniques are the alphabet; forms assembled from them make sense far faster when the letters are already familiar.

## How to answer

- Lead with the three-question framework, then the table — a user who internalizes the framework never needs the list re-explained.
- For "what's the difference between X and Y" questions, use the look-alike pairs table and name the single distinguishing feature (blade part, direction, or wrist).
- Keep pinyin with the English names — practitioners hear both in class, and the pair (dian = point) is the searchable hook.
- For beginners, recommend learning the 13 before or alongside the first form; for users mid-form, show them how to name techniques inside postures they already know.
- Note the lineage-variant caveat when a user's school list differs — match by description, not by count.
