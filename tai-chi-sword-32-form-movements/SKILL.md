## The list by section (the way to learn it)

The form was deliberately designed as four sections of eight movements. Memorize one section at a time.

## Read the names as instructions

The poetic names are a teaching system, not decoration: "Dragonfly Touches the Water" prescribes a light, precise touch; "Black Dragon Whips His Tail" prescribes committed power. When a user's execution feels wrong, have them re-read the name and ask what quality it demands — the imagery usually corrects the movement faster than mechanical cues.

## Naming-variant notes (prevent confusion)

- **English names vary between schools and books.** The sequence is standardized; the translations are not. "Gauge the Depth of the Sea" may appear as "Probe the Ocean Bottom"; "Resting Bird Enters the Forest" as "Bird Returning to the Woods." Match by position number, not by wording.
- **The opening salute is usually unnumbered.** Most performances begin with a preparation/opening posture before movement 1; some lists number it, which shifts every number by one. When comparing lists, check whether an opening is counted.
- **The movements train 7 core sword techniques (jianfa):** dian (point), ci (thrust), dai (carry), pi (chop), chou (withdraw), ti (lift), ji (hit) — the foundation vocabulary every later form builds on.
- **Background:** the 32-form was created in 1957 under the Chinese National Sports Commission (led by master Li Tianji) as a simplified Yang-style set for mass teaching. It is the most widely practiced tai chi sword routine in the world.

## How to memorize the sequence

1. **Eight at a time.** Learn section 1 until it runs without thinking before touching section 2. Four chunks of eight beat one chunk of thirty-two.
2. **Say the names aloud while moving.** Name + image + motion binds far better than motion alone.
3. **Use the story logic.** Within each section the names flow as images — dragonfly, dipper, swallow; ape, dust, boat, meteor. Recalling the image chain recalls the order.
4. **Expect 3–6 months** for the full sequence at one class per week with home practice; refinement continues long after the names are memorized.

## FAQ

### What are all the names of the 32-form tai chi sword movements?
In order: Dragonfly Touches the Water, The Big Dipper, Swallow Skims the Water, Block and Sweep Right, Block and Sweep Left, Gauge the Depth of the Sea, Hold the Moon Against the Chest, Resting Bird Enters the Forest, Black Dragon Whips His Tail, Blue Dragon Out of Water, Wind Blows the Lotus Leaf, Lion Shakes Its Head, Tiger Holds Its Head, Wild Horse Jumps Over the Stream, Lift Sword in Left Empty Stance, Lift Sword in Right Bow Stance, Shoot the Wild Geese, White Ape Presents Fruit, Clean Up Dust in the Wind (left, right, left), Push the Boat Downstream, Shooting Star Chases the Moon, Swallow Picks Up Mud, Raise the Screen, Left Wheel Sword, Right Wheel Sword, Phoenix Spreads Its Wings, Step Back and Lift Sword, Fish the Moon from the Sea Bottom, The Spirit Cat Catches the Rat, Closing Form.

### How many movements are in the 32-form tai chi sword?
32 numbered movements, organized in four sections of eight, preceded by a short opening posture that most lists leave unnumbered. A full performance runs about 3–4 minutes.

### Why does my school use different names for the 32-form movements?
English translations of the standardized Chinese sequence vary between schools, books, and videos. The movements and their order are fixed; only the English wording differs. Compare by movement number, and check whether the list counts the opening posture.

### Who created the 32-form tai chi sword?
It was standardized in 1957 by the Chinese National Sports Commission under master Li Tianji, distilled from traditional Yang-style sword routines as a simplified set for mass teaching.

### How long does it take to learn the 32-form tai chi sword?
Roughly 3–6 months to learn the full sequence at one class per week plus home practice; the rest of the first year is spent refining blade line and flow rather than memorizing.

### What should I learn after the 32-form?
The 42-form competition set (created 1989, blends four styles) for breadth and tournaments, or a traditional style set for depth. Most students benefit from a full year of refining the 32-form first.
